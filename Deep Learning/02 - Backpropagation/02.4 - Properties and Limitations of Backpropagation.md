## Properties

Backpropagation has several important characteristics that define both its strengths and its practical constraints:

| Property | Description |
|---|---|
| **Computationally efficient** | Only requires matrix multiplications and simple thresholding (e.g. ReLU). Much cheaper than perturbing individual weights. |
| **Memory hungry** | All intermediate quantities (activations, pre-activations) computed during the forward pass must be stored to compute gradients during the backward pass. |
| **Parallelisable across batches** | Multiple samples can be processed simultaneously, but distribution across machines becomes difficult if the full model does not fit on a single device. |

## Problems

Despite its efficiency, backpropagation has fundamental limitations that distinguish it from biological learning and constrain its applicability.

### Problem 1 — Detachment of Learning and Inference

To update the weights of a neural network, the training algorithm must **fully stop inference** in order to perform backpropagation. This creates a hard separation between the learning phase and the prediction phase.

> In the real world, the brain receives a **constant stream of information** and learns continuously — it does not pause perception to update its parameters.

This detachment makes backpropagation biologically implausible and creates challenges for **online** or **continual learning** scenarios.

### Problem 2 — Requirement of Differentiability

Backpropagation relies on computing gradients via the chain rule. This means that **every operation in the forward pass must be differentiable** (or at least have a well-defined sub-gradient).

If any component of the computation graph is non-differentiable (e.g. a hard discrete decision, a non-smooth lookup, or a stochastic discrete sample), the gradient signal cannot flow through it and standard backpropagation breaks down.
