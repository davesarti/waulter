## Setup — One Neuron per Layer

To understand the mathematics of backpropagation, we start with the simplest possible network: **one neuron per layer**.

![[Pasted image 20260702122308.png|256]]

Each layer $l$ computes two quantities:

$$z^{(l)} = w^{(l)}\, a^{(l-1)} + b^{(l)}$$

$$a^{(l)} = \sigma\!\left(z^{(l)}\right)$$

where $w^{(l)}$ is the weight, $b^{(l)}$ is the bias, $a^{(l-1)}$ is the activation from the previous layer, and $\sigma$ is a non-linear activation function (e.g. ReLU or sigmoid).

---

## The Backward Step — Applying the Chain Rule

Our goal during the backward pass is to compute $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(l)}}$: **how sensitive the loss is to a small change in the weight**.

A small change in $w^{(l)}$ causes a change in $z^{(l)}$, which in turn causes a change in $a^{(l)}$, which directly affects $L$. This dependency chain is exactly what the **chain rule** captures:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(l)}} = \underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(l)}}{\partial w^{(l)}}}_{\text{how } w^{(l)} \text{ changes } z^{(l)}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial a^{(l)}}{\partial z^{(l)}}}_{\text{how } z^{(l)} \text{ changes } a^{(l)}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(l)}}}_{\text{how } a^{(l)} \text{ changes } L}$$

The same logic applies to the bias term (use $b$ instead of $w$ in the formula above).

---

## Propagating to Earlier Layers

For weights in earlier layers, the influence on $L$ is indirect — it passes through all subsequent layers. We first ask: *how sensitive is $L$ to the activation of the previous layer?*

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(l-1)}} = \frac{\partial z^{(l)}}{\partial a^{(l-1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a^{(l)}}{\partial z^{(l)}} \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(l)}}$$

This quantity $\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(l-1)}}$ then becomes the "upstream gradient" for the layer below. For a weight $w^{(l-1)}$ two layers back, the full chain expands to:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(l-1)}} = \frac{\partial z^{(l-1)}}{\partial w^{(l-1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a^{(l-1)}}{\partial z^{(l-1)}} \cdot \underbrace{\frac{\partial z^{(l)}}{\partial a^{(l-1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a^{(l)}}{\partial z^{(l)}} \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial a^{(l)}}}_{\partial L / \partial a^{(l-1)}}$$

By following the dependency tree and multiplying partial derivatives along the path, we can compute the gradient of the loss with respect to **any weight or bias** in the network. This is the chain rule — applied repeatedly.

Once all gradients are known, the full gradient vector is:

$$\Delta_w L = \left[\frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(1)}},\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(1)}},\ \ldots,\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial w^{(l)}},\ \frac{\partial L}{\partial b^{(l)}}\right]$$

---

## Extending to Multiple Neurons per Layer

When propagating back to a previous-layer activation $a_i^{(l-1)}$, neuron $i$ influences **all** neurons $j$ in the next layer simultaneously. The total sensitivity must sum over all those pathways:

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_i^{(l-1)}} = \sum_j \frac{\partial z_j^{(l)}}{\partial a_i^{(l-1)}} \cdot \frac{\partial a_j^{(l)}}{\partial z_j^{(l)}} \cdot \frac{\partial L}{\partial a_j^{(l)}}$$


---

## From Scalars to Matrices

The scalar derivation above works in principle, but operating weight-by-weight is impractical. In real implementations, all weights and biases are handled simultaneously via **matrix operations**, enabling efficient parallelism on modern hardware.
