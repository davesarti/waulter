## The Gradient Descent Algorithm

Training a neural network means finding the parameters $\mathbf{W}$ that minimize the loss $\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})$. **Gradient Descent** does this iteratively by always stepping in the direction of **steepest descent** — the negative gradient.

### Update Rule

$$\mathbf{W} \leftarrow \mathbf{W} - \eta\, \nabla_{\mathbf{W}} \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{W})$$

where $\eta$ is the **learning rate** controlling the step size, and the gradient vector is:

$$\nabla_{\mathbf{W}} \mathcal{L} = \left[\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1},\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2},\, \ldots,\, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_N}\right]$$

Each partial derivative $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_i}$ measures **how fast the loss changes** in the direction of $w_i$. When the gradient is zero, the loss is not changing in any direction.

The algorithm runs until **convergence is reached** or a predefined number of iterations is completed.

## Challenges

### Local Minima

The loss landscape of neural networks is **non-convex**, meaning multiple local minima exist. However, local minima are often still effective solutions in practice and are not typically a significant concern during training.

### Saddle Points

At a **saddle point** the gradient is zero even though the point is not a minimum — some directions curve upward, others downward. Saddle points are common in high-dimensional spaces but only become problematic if the optimizer gets stuck exactly on one.
