Transformers are a novel neural network architecture introduced in the paper *"Attention Is All You Need"* (Vaswani et al.). They are based on a **self-attention mechanism**, forming the backbone of neural machine translation architectures that use fully connected (FC) layers and attention.

Compared to recurrent or convolutional architectures (see [[08.1 - CNNs in sequence models|CNNs in sequence models]]), Transformers are more efficient and easier to parallelize. This characteristic makes them faster to train and capable of handling larger datasets effectively.

## Self-Attention

**Self-Attention** is a mechanism that captures the context of other words relevant to the one we are currently processing (but in practice we do it in parallel for all the words!).

To calculate self-attention, we start by creating three vectors from the embeddings: **query** ($Q$), **key** ($K$) and **value** ($V$). These vectors are obtained by multiplying the word embeddings ($X$) by three different weight matrices ($W^Q$, $W^K$ and $W^V$) learned during the training process:

- $Q$: the query represents the current word for which we are searching for relevant words -> what the word needs to match
- $K$: the key represents the potentially relevant words -> what each word look like to other word
- $V$: the value represents the content of the relevant words.

The self-attention formula is as follows:
$$\text{Attention}(Q, K, V) = \text{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) V$$

The scalar product between query and key ($QK^T$) is called **score** and measures how similar two words are.
To obtain stable scores, we divide it by the square root of the size of the keys ($d_k$) and apply softmax.
We multiply each value ($V$) by the softmax score, keeping the values of important words and reducing those of irrelevant words. We then sum these weighted values to get the self-attention output ($Z$).

> [!info] Soft Attention is Differentiable
> Soft attention is differentiable, making it easy to train the model by backpropagation.

## Multi-Head Attention

In Transformers, we use **multi-head attention** to improve the model's ability to focus on different positions in the sentence and provide the attention level with more "subspaces of representation". 

In multi-head attention, vectors of input embeddings are divided into several separate **"heads"** (usually 8, in the classical Transformer). Each head operates on its own linear projections of query, key and value. After calculating the attention for each head, the results are **concatenated and multiplied by an additional weight matrix** to produce the final attention level output.

## Positional Encoding

The Transformer has no intrinsic sense of the order or position of words within a sentence. To compensate for this lack, we use **positional encoding**.

When we combine word embeddings with positional encoding in the context of Transformer models, we obtain **"time-signal embeddings"**. This means that each embedding vector not only represents the meaning of the word, but also includes a component that indicates its relative position in the input sequence.

**Sinusoidal functions** are used to allow different positions in the embedding vector to be represented.

### Encoder-Decoder

The classical Transformer architecture is composed of two main components:

- **Encoder**: Each input word is transformed into a high-dimensional vector representation. It mainly consists of two modules, the **Multi-Head Attention** and the **Feed-Forward Network**, which processes the output vectors of the multi-head attention. This layer is independent along different paths, enabling parallel execution.
- **Decoder**: generates the output sequence one word at a time. It consists of three separate modules: the **Masked Multi-Head Attention**, the **Multi-Head Attention**, and the **Feed-Forward Network**.
  - The **Masked Multi-Head Attention** ensures that each position in the sequence can only attend to previous positions, and not future ones, thereby allowing the decoder to generate the sequence step-by-step without peeking ahead.
  - The additional **Multi-Head Attention** module operates on the outputs of the Encoder stack, allowing the decoder to focus on different parts of the encoder input, improving the model's ability to capture information relevant to output generation.

In both the encoder and the decoder, $n\times$ identical layers are stacked ($n=6$ in the original paper). **Residual connections** and **[[Layer Normalization]]** ("Add & Norm") are used to provide greater stability during training and to facilitate the flow of gradients through the various layers of the architecture.

Before being fed to the encoder/decoder stacks, inputs and outputs are embedded and combined with positional encoding to form "time-signal embeddings".

## Conclusion

After the advent of Transformers, the community began exploring models that utilize **either the encoder or decoder independently**:
- Encoder-only models leverage output hidden states, which can serve as features in broader models designed for diverse tasks (e.g., BERT).
- Decoder-only models, specifically designed for text generation, prove highly effective for applications such as machine translation or summarization (e.g., the GPT series).
